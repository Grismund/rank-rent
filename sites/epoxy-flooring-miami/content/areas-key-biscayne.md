## What Drives Epoxy Demand in Key Biscayne

### Climate and Concrete Conditions

Key Biscayne faces the most extreme moisture conditions in the Miami-Dade service area. As a barrier island sitting directly on porous oolitic limestone, the water table can reach as shallow as 12 inches below grade during the wet season. Hydrostatic pressure pushes moisture through concrete slabs from May through October in a cycle that never stops. Slab moisture vapor transmission in coastal Miami-Dade averages 4 to 8 pounds per 1,000 square feet per 24 hours — well above the 3-pound threshold for standard epoxy adhesion.

The island's surrounded-by-water geography pushes local humidity above the Miami-Dade average of 73 percent. Humidity on Key Biscayne rarely drops below 65 percent even during the dry season. At 70 percent relative humidity, epoxy tensile strength drops by 25 percent. Amine blush — a waxy film that forms on uncured epoxy in humid conditions — destroys the bond between coats and requires sanding before recoating.

Salt exposure on Key Biscayne is direct and constant. The Atlantic Ocean lies to the east, Biscayne Bay to the west. Chloride ions from sea spray penetrate unsealed concrete and corrode embedded rebar. That rust occupies two to six times the volume of the original steel, cracking slabs from within. Efflorescence and spalling are common indicators on the island's concrete surfaces and demand thorough surface preparation — including diamond grinding — before any epoxy application.
