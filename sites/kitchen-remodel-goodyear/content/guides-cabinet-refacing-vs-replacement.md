Deciding between **cabinet refacing vs. replacing** is one of the first choices Goodyear homeowners face when planning a kitchen update. The answer depends on the condition of the existing boxes, the scope of the remodel, and how much of the budget you want to allocate to cabinets versus other upgrades. This guide walks through the differences so you can make an informed decision before calling a contractor.

## Quick Summary

**Refacing** means keeping the existing cabinet boxes and replacing the doors, drawer fronts, hardware, and sometimes the face frames with a veneer. The layout stays the same. The kitchen remains partially functional during the work. The project takes three to five days for a standard kitchen and costs between $4,000 and $8,000.

**Replacing** means removing the old cabinets entirely and installing new ones. It allows layout changes — moving the sink, adding an island, reconfiguring the cabinet arrangement. The kitchen goes out of service during demolition and installation. The project takes two to four weeks (longer if countertops and other surfaces are changing at the same time) and costs between $8,000 and $20,000 or more for custom cabinetry.

The core trade-off: refacing delivers a visual refresh at lower cost and less disruption, but it cannot fix a bad layout or structurally failed boxes. Replacement costs more and takes longer, but it gives you a clean slate.

## Key Differences

**Cost.** Refacing runs roughly 40 to 60 percent of full replacement cost. For a standard Goodyear kitchen, that translates to $4,000 to $8,000 for refacing versus $8,000 to $20,000 for new cabinets. The savings come from keeping the boxes — the most labor-intensive and material-heavy part of cabinet work — in place.

**Timeline.** Refacing is measured in days. Replacement is measured in weeks. If the replacement is part of a larger remodel that includes countertops, plumbing, and electrical, the cabinet portion may overlap with other trades, but the kitchen will be unusable for a longer stretch.

**Scope of change.** Refacing changes what you see: doors, drawer fronts, hardware, and exposed face frames. It does not change the layout, the number of cabinets, the shelf configuration, or the internal storage. Replacement lets you redesign the entire cabinet arrangement — add a lazy Susan corner, convert a base cabinet to a pull-out trash bin, raise the uppers to the ceiling, or add cabinets where there were none.

**Structural requirements.** Refacing only works when the cabinet boxes are in good condition. If the boxes are warped, water-damaged, delaminating, or sagging under load, they need to be replaced, not veneered. Many Goodyear homes built in the mid-2000s used particle-board boxes with melamine coating. These hold up well for 15 to 20 years under normal conditions but deteriorate faster if exposed to water leaks — a dishwasher overflow or a slow sink drain can ruin the base cabinets without being obvious from the outside.

**Permits.** Pure refacing (no plumbing, electrical, or structural changes) does not require a permit in Goodyear. Cabinet replacement that is part of a larger remodel involving plumbing or electrical changes does require a permit from the Development Services Department.

**Disruption.** During refacing, the cabinet boxes and shelves remain in place. You may be able to use the kitchen with limited access to certain cabinets. During replacement, the kitchen is stripped bare for at least several days during demolition and installation.

## How This Affects Your Decision

Start with two questions:

**Are the boxes in good structural condition?** Open a few base and upper cabinets. Check the shelves for sagging. Look at the bottom of the base cabinets near the dishwasher and sink — if the material is swollen, soft, or discolored, that is water damage. Run your hand along the face frames. If they feel solid and square, refacing is an option. If they flex, bow, or show damage, replacement is the safer path.

**Does the layout need to change?** If you are satisfied with where everything sits — the sink, the stove, the refrigerator, the storage arrangement — and the only problem is the appearance, refacing solves it at a fraction of the cost. If the layout is the issue — not enough counter space, no island, cabinets in the wrong configuration, or a closed-off galley that you want to open — replacement is the only way to address it.

For Goodyear homeowners with 2000s-era builder-grade kitchens, the most common scenario is structurally sound boxes with an outdated door style. Honey oak raised-panel doors, dark cherry stain, and ornate hardware were standard in the mid-2000s. The boxes behind those doors are usually fine. Refacing to white or gray shaker doors with modern pulls transforms the kitchen for $4,000 to $8,000, leaving budget for a [countertop replacement](/countertop-replacement/) or other upgrades.

If the boxes are compromised, or if the homeowner wants layout changes, a [full kitchen remodel](/full-kitchen-remodel/) that includes cabinet replacement is the better investment even though it costs more and takes longer. Trying to reface boxes that need replacing leads to premature failure and wasted money.

A third option exists for homeowners somewhere in between: replace a few damaged base cabinets (especially under the sink and dishwasher) while refacing the rest. Some contractors offer this hybrid approach. It costs more than pure refacing but less than full replacement.
