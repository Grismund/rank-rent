## About Commercial Epoxy Flooring

The chemistry is the same as residential work — resin mixed with hardener — but commercial systems are specified heavier and tailored to the conditions they will face. Builds run thicker (20 to 60 mils versus 10 to 20 for a garage), cure schedules are managed more carefully, and the coating can carry specialized properties: anti-static for electronics warehouses, USDA-compliant finishes for food preparation, or chemical resistance matched to whatever a facility handles.

Thickness is the core difference. A garage floor handles parked cars. A commercial floor handles forklift loads, pallet jacks, and thousands of footsteps per week. The extra build is what allows it to absorb that level of use without wearing through to bare concrete at the loading dock or the high-traffic entrance.

## Advantages of Commercial Epoxy Flooring

What a commercial system addresses:

- **Abrasion.** Hard wheels and heavy loads grind uncoated concrete into dust. A commercial epoxy absorbs daily mechanical wear.
- **Chemical exposure.** Oils, degreasers, solvents, and cleaning agents stay on the surface where they can be removed.
- **Safety.** Anti-slip aggregate broadcast into the topcoat where floors stay wet. Traffic lanes, pedestrian walkways, and hazard zones marked directly into the coating instead of painted over it.
- **Maintenance.** No grout, no pores, no joints collecting debris. Sweep and mop.
- **Presentation.** A restaurant floor on Sand Lake Road, an auto showroom on Colonial Drive, or a brewery taproom in the Milk District all benefit from a floor that looks deliberately finished.

The right system depends entirely on what the floor has to survive. A contractor experienced in commercial work will specify it based on your conditions rather than applying the same product they use in residential garages.

## The Commercial Epoxy Flooring Process

The stages parallel residential installations. The materials, scheduling, and project scale do not. Most commercial jobs in Orlando are phased around business hours — sometimes overnight — so operations continue.

### Concrete Preparation

Preparation is the bulk of the labor. Crews grind or shot-blast the entire slab to strip old coatings, adhesive residue, and whatever has soaked into the concrete over the facility's lifetime, leaving a profile the new coating can grip.

On older commercial slabs, joint work is standard. Expansion joints typically remain as functional joints; control joints get filled with semi-rigid filler to create a smooth, continuous coating surface. A moisture vapor test follows. Orlando commercial slabs, particularly in older structures without modern vapor barriers, can read above manufacturer thresholds due to the shallow water table.

### Epoxy Application

Primer goes down first, often a moisture-mitigating formulation on slabs that test above the manufacturer's limit. The base coat follows in one or more passes to reach the specified thickness.

Color zones, safety markings, and traffic lanes get incorporated into the coating during application rather than added afterward. Anti-slip grit goes into the topcoat wherever water, grease, or food waste will be a daily presence: commercial kitchens, wash bays, and loading dock aprons.

### Curing and Final Coat

The final layer is typically polyurethane or polyaspartic, selected for UV stability, abrasion performance, and tire-mark resistance. Light foot traffic is generally safe after about 24 hours. Full operational loads — forklifts, stacking equipment, delivery trucks — require five or more days. An experienced contractor provides a phased return-to-service schedule so you can reopen sections of the floor in sequence rather than shutting down entirely.
